Abstract

This assignment asks for a strategy engine that plans routes for three drones over a 50 × 50 disaster grid. Each drone must start from a good deployment cell, visit waypoints, choose a speed and a sensor power, and avoid obstacles, no-fly zones and dangerous cells. We use a genetic algorithm (GA) because it can search many possible plans and balance several goals at the same time. A chromosome stores each drone's deployment cell, every waypoint, its speed and its sensor choice. A simulator flies the plan and scores it using high-priority zone coverage, target detection, energy, flight time and hazard exposure. We compare the GA with random search using the same number of trials. Over three seeds, the GA scored 102.9 ± 6.6 on average, while random search scored 78.4 ± 5.9. The GA covered 80.6% of the priority zones and found 13.3 of 14 targets; random search covered 65.0% and found 11.3 targets. The tuned GA reached 84.4% coverage and detected all targets. This report explains the design, tests the main parameters, and shows coverage heatmaps across generations.

Code and results: github.com/ChTayyabMehmood/uav-strategy-engine

1. Introduction

After a disaster, a small drone team must inspect the affected area quickly. Some cells are blocked, some are unsafe, and some target zones are more important than others. The team also has limited battery and flight time. A good plan must balance all of these goals, so there is no single correct route.

This report presents a simple and reproducible strategy engine. Section 2 explains how a plan is represented and scored. Section 3 gives the experiment setup. Section 4 compares the GA with random search and shows how coverage changes across generations. Section 5 tests population size, mutation rate and selection pressure. Section 6 discusses the result and its limits.

2. System Design

2.1 Chromosome representation

One chromosome describes the whole fleet. It has two segments. The deployment segment stores one starting cell for each of the three drones. The route segment stores three blocks, one per drone, and each block has six waypoints. A waypoint is a pair of whole-number grid coordinates (x, y), a speed level (0 = slow, 1 = normal, 2 = fast) and a sensor level (0 = off, 1 = medium, 2 = strong). The drone starts at its deployment cell and visits the waypoints in order. The speed and sensor values of a waypoint apply to the leg that leads to it. In total, one chromosome stores 3 × 2 deployment values and 3 × 6 × 4 waypoint values = 78 genes.

We chose this hybrid design for three reasons. First, grid coordinates are easy to check and repair. Second, speed and sensor choices are naturally discrete. Third, keeping the deployment cell and its route in the same block lets crossover move a complete drone plan without separating a route from the base that was evolved with it.

2.2 Fitness function

The score rewards coverage (C) and target detection (D), and punishes energy (E), time (T) and risk (R). Each term is scaled between 0 and 1, so the weights show their relative importance:

score = 100C + 50D − 20E − 15T − 30R − P (1)

C is the share of high-priority zone mass surveyed by the active sensors. Every target owns a circular priority zone; covering the whole zone contributes its full weight, so this is an area-coverage measure, not just a count of visited target points. D is the share of the 14 targets detected. E is total energy divided by the fleet limit (780), T is the longest drone flight time divided by the time limit (120), and R is the average hazard level on the cells that were flown over. P is a penalty for a plan that breaks a hard constraint.

Coverage has the largest reward (+100) because surveying the important zones is the main task. Detection is next (+50) so that the plan is also rewarded for finding each target. Risk has the largest punishment (−30) because flying through fire or radiation is dangerous. Energy (−20) and time (−15) have smaller weights because the hard limits already reject plans that run out of battery or exceed the range; these weights only prefer safer margins among valid plans.

2.3 Genetic operators and constraints

Selection uses a tournament of three plans: pick three at random and keep the best. Crossover takes two parents and swaps the last part of their drone blocks, including each block's deployment cell and route, so the structure stays valid. Mutation visits each waypoint with probability 0.20 and then moves the waypoint, changes its speed, or changes its sensor; each deployment cell is also relocated with probability 0.05. The two best valid plans are copied to the next generation unchanged. If the best score does not improve for four generations, 10% random immigrants replace the worst plans. This keeps the population diverse and helps avoid premature convergence.

A repair step runs before scoring. It pulls waypoints back inside the grid, moves them out of obstacles and no-fly cells, and repairs a leg that crosses a blocked cell. If repair cannot fix the plan, the penalty P is added. The penalty is 1000 plus 50 for each violation and 25 for each unit of violation, so an invalid plan cannot beat a valid one. The evaluator independently checks every leg and rejects a plan that exceeds range or battery limits. The GA stops after 50 generations.

3. Experiment Setup

The simulation uses a 50 × 50 grid, 3 drones, 6 waypoints per drone and 14 targets. The GA uses 60 plans per generation for 50 generations, which gives 3,000 evaluations with caching. Random search also receives 3,000 evaluations. Both methods use the same simulator and score function. We repeat each method for seeds 42, 43 and 44 to reduce the effect of luck.

4. Results and Comparison

Table 1 shows that the GA gives a much better mission plan. Its average score is 102.9, which is 31% higher than the random-search score of 78.4. It covers 15.6 more percentage points of priority zones and finds two more targets on average. It also needs less flight time (72.4 versus 79.5) and has lower risk (0.225 versus 0.272). Random search uses less energy on average (348.9 versus 372.9), because it often takes short, direct routes; the GA instead spends energy to reach more priority zones. This is the expected trade-off in a multi-objective problem.

The GA won on every seed. Its three scores were 101.3, 110.1 and 97.2, while random search scored 85.2, 73.9 and 76.3. The worst GA run was still better than the best random run. The tuned run (population 200, tournament 5, 10,000 evaluations) reached 109.4, covered 84.4% of the priority zones and detected all 14 targets. The equal-budget result is the fair comparison; the tuned result shows what more search can add.

5. Hyperparameter Sensitivity

We tested one parameter at a time with two seeds and 30 generations. The results are shown in Figure 3. A population of 50 scored 101.5, a population of 100 scored 104.5, and a population of 200 scored 105.1. The largest population gave a small gain but needed about 2.3 times the run time of 100, so 100 is the best practical balance.

For mutation, 0.10 scored 103.4, 0.20 scored 103.0 and 0.30 scored 102.4. The three values are close, which means the GA is not fragile: moderate mutation is enough, while very large changes waste good plans. For tournament size, 2 scored 101.6, 3 scored 103.0 and 5 scored 106.9. Stronger selection pressure worked best here because the immigrant step still protects diversity.

6. Discussion

The final plan is not unique. Different seeds produced different deployment cells and routes, and all of them were valid. This is expected: many plans can visit the same zones in a different order, and a different base can make the same route cheaper. The GA therefore gives the team a set of good options, not one fixed answer. A human planner could choose a plan that also avoids a school or a hospital, even if another plan has a slightly higher score.

Random search is simpler, but most random plans are poor. The GA reuses good parts of plans through selection and crossover, and the deployment genes let it learn where the fleet should start. In this experiment, the GA won on score, zone coverage, detection, flight time and risk. Random search used less energy, which shows the trade-off clearly rather than hiding it.

The main limitation is that the map is simulated. The drones do not face wind, moving hazards or loss of communication, and the score weights are a design choice. A real system should test more seeds, use live map data and let a human check the final plan.

7. Conclusion

This project built a complete GA strategy engine for a three-drone disaster mission. The chromosome stores deployment cells, waypoints, speed and sensor choices; the score balances zone coverage, detection, energy, time and risk; and repair keeps plans valid. The GA beat random search with the same budget, and the best run found every target with 84.4% priority-zone coverage. The coverage heatmaps show how the plan improves across generations, and the result is one of several good routes, which is the right outcome for a problem with competing goals.
